\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Beautification hurts every detector, in both directions}
\label{sec:measure}
Table~\ref{tab:twosided} scores the thirteen detectors and our models by the rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol} on the three
sources. Every published detector loses AUC under beautification on every source. The loss is largest for the detectors
that detect best: on Celeb-DF-B the AUC of SBI falls from 0.875 to 0.752, of Effort from 0.909 to 0.807 and of Forensics
Adapter from 0.909 to 0.828, and after the re-encoding of the same videos is subtracted the app presets alone raise their
miss rates by 21.8, 11.0 and 15.2 points. The same two CLIP detectors fall from 0.88--0.89 to 0.79 under the eleven
Celeb-DF-v2 filters, where their miss rates rise by 16.8 and 12.1 points, and from 0.94 to 0.90--0.91 under the sixteen DFD
filters, built after our method was fixed, where Effort's miss rate rises by 12.3 points [8.3, 15.7]. The false-alarm side
is smaller for these two on the colour filters (Effort $-$0.5 to $+$1.2, Forensics Adapter $+$2.9 to $+$5.2 points) and
large for UnivFD (+8.9 and +17.1) and NPR (+69 on Celeb-DF-v2, whose scores collapse under any filter). On commercial
retouching the false alarms dominate: at a threshold that accuses 5\% of the untouched originals, the eleven FF++-trained
detectors accuse 36--63\% of the most strongly smoothed genuine faces (Sec.~\ref{sec:ali}).

Two findings about the published numbers matter for reading the table. First, Celeb-DF-B's untreated AUCs (0.81--0.91) are
below the Celeb-DF-v2 numbers the detectors' papers report, because our frames are cropped by one pipeline for every
detector (supplement, Table~S1 lists each detector's published number and ours); the beautification effect is measured
within each detector on the same crops and does not depend on this. Second, a fixed threshold sees what AUC cannot. Our
RU5 model keeps its AUC under the DFD filters (0.942 to 0.927) while its miss rate rises by 12.0 points, because the filters
shift its scores down for genuine and forged frames alike; a deployed detector would let those forgeries through. We report
both numbers for every model.

\subsection{Where the damage comes from}
\label{sec:mechanism}
We take the FF++-only CLIP model, whose forgery decision is a linear head $w$ on the pooled feature, and measure how each
of the eleven held-out Celeb-DF-v2 filters moves the feature of a validation frame (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). The two
filters with a radial luminance overlay, \emph{earlybird} and \emph{mayfair}, move the forgery logit of a genuine frame by
$+7.8$ and $+5.6$; the nine pure colour looks move it by $+0.1$ to $+2.4$. The mean shifts of the two overlay filters have
cosines of 0.20 and 0.33 with $w$, those of the colour looks 0.00 to 0.09, and the shift of \emph{earlybird} is as long as
the feature itself. Forged frames move much less ($-0.7$ to $+1.8$). A luminance ramp around the face reads as forgery
evidence, and it does so for a genuine face far more than for a forged one, which already carries that evidence.

The shifts are not new directions. For every held-out filter, 63--90\% of the shift energy lies in the 16-dimensional
subspace spanned by the shifts that training-time beautifications (random draws of the operator and random grading) produce
on training frames. What the model had not seen is the magnitude and the combination. This suggests a linear fix: project
the subspace out of the feature and refit the head. It fails (Table~\ref{tab:erasure}). Removing two directions costs the
clean AUC at once (0.987 to 0.933; clean miss rate 6\% to 26\%) and the false-alarm rate under the held-out filters does not
fall (17.5--27.5\% against 21.5\% for the trained head), because the directions that beautification moves along are the
directions that carry the forgery evidence. The two have to be separated during training, which is what Sec.~\ref{sec:robust}
does.
